# Midterm Project: AI System Audit Through a Neuroscience Lens

## Problem
The purpose of this midterm project was to evaluate a modern artificial intelligence system through concepts learned in neuroscience. The project focused on comparing biological cognition with artificial intelligence by designing experiments related to memory, perception, attention, and processing architecture. The goal was to determine where current AI systems resemble biological intelligence and where important differences still remain.

## Approach
For this project, I selected Google Gemini as the AI system under evaluation. I designed a series of neuroscience-inspired experiments based on concepts from multiple course modules, including memory systems, sensory perception, attention, and parallel versus sequential processing.

The experiments tested how Gemini handled contextual memory, multitasking demands, information filtering, and perception-like reasoning tasks. I compared Gemini’s behavior to biological mechanisms discussed throughout the course, including working memory, reconstructive recall, distributed processing, selective attention, and perceptual interpretation.

To support the analysis, I documented prompts, outputs, observations, and comparisons between expected biological behavior and the responses generated by the AI system.

## Results
The project demonstrated that Google Gemini can simulate many behaviors associated with cognition, including contextual reasoning, summarization, pattern recognition, and conversational continuity within a session. However, the experiments also showed important limitations compared to biological intelligence.

Gemini performed best when tasks were clearly structured or sequentially organized, while more complex simultaneous instructions sometimes resulted in dropped constraints, blended tasks, or reduced coherence. The system also demonstrated limitations in persistent memory and perceptual interpretation, relying more on language pattern prediction than true sensory understanding or long-term adaptive memory.

Overall, the audit showed that while large language models can approximate aspects of human cognition, they remain fundamentally different from biological neural systems in areas such as temporal processing, embodied perception, adaptive memory consolidation, and autonomous attention control.

## What I Learned
This project helped me better understand how neuroscience concepts can be used to critically evaluate modern AI systems rather than simply measure performance outputs. I learned that many AI capabilities that appear “intelligent” are often the result of statistical prediction and language modeling rather than biologically grounded cognition.

I also gained experience designing controlled experiments, evaluating AI limitations, and connecting course theories such as Thousand Brains Theory, Global Workspace Theory, and sensory processing models to real-world AI behavior. The project reinforced that neuroscience continues to provide valuable insight into both the strengths and shortcomings of current artificial intelligence systems.
